**Theorem.** For the ordered Akbulut–Kirby pair

$$R_n=(x^n y^{-(n+1)},\;xyx(yxy)^{-1}),$$

the minimum number of conjugations in an ordinary Andrews–Curtis trivialization of $R_1$ is **exactly two**. The shortest total length is **exactly nine**. One path simultaneously attains all three minima with five right multiplications, two inversions, and two conjugations. More generally, for every $n\ge1$, any ordinary path from $R_n$ to the ordered pair $(x,y)$ requires **at least two conjugations and at least $n+6$ moves in total**.

We use the three constructors of the official rank-preserving `AC.Step`. A move inverts one relator, right-multiplies it by the other relator with positive sign, or conjugates it by an arbitrary free-group word. Each conjugation counts once, regardless of the length of its conjugator. The result gives a necessary condition on any trivialization of $R_n$. The general statement bounds every path that exists. At $n=1$ the uniform length bound gives seven, and the separate optimality argument raises this to the exact value nine. [Official move definitions](https://github.com/SAIRcompetition/Andrews-Curtis/blob/a0fd6e6f52d82c93ccc06ab91d81e8fa3678256e/competition/tools/lean/AC.lean)

**An elementary obstruction for $R_1$.** Map $x$ to $X=(0\;2\;1\;3)$ and $y$ to $Y=(0\;1\;3\;2)$ in $S_4$, composing right to left. Direct multiplication gives $XY^{-2}=(2\;3)=t$ and $XYX=YXY$. Both relators therefore start in $\{1,t\}$. Inversion and multiplication keep them there. A single conjugation can introduce only one new transposition, a conjugate of $t$. All subsequent conjugation-free moves stay in the subgroup generated by these two transpositions. Draw their two edges on four vertices. The resulting graph is disconnected, so every element of the subgroup preserves its components. The target contains the four-cycle $X$, which is transitive. This excludes every path with zero or one conjugation, with no bound on the number of intervening moves.

**A path attaining two.** Number the slots 0 and 1. Write $I_i$ for inversion, $M_{ij}$ for $r_i\gets r_ir_j$, and $C_i(w)$ for $r_i\gets wr_iw^{-1}$. The following list is chronological.

$$I_0,\ M_{10},\ C_1(y^{-1}x^{-1}),\ M_{10},\ C_1(xyx^{-1}),\ I_0,\ M_{10},\ M_{01},\ M_{01}.$$

The first move changes the first relator to $y^2x^{-1}$. During moves 2 through 5 the successive second relators reduce to $xyxy^{-1}x^{-1}yx^{-1}$, $xy^{-1}x^{-1}y^2$, $xy^{-1}x^{-1}y^4x^{-1}$, and $y^3x^{-1}$. Move 6 restores the first relator to $xy^{-2}$. The last three products give $(xy^{-2},y)$, $(xy^{-1},y)$, and $(x,y)$. Thus the lower and upper bounds on the conjugation count agree.

**Why nine moves are necessary.** In the same $S_4$ image, inversions and conjugations preserve cycle type. The starting types are a transposition and the identity. Until the second slot is multiplied by the first, the types remain unchanged. At that first effective product both slots become transpositions. To finish with two four-cycles, each slot must be multiplied again. If only two products remain, they must update distinct slots. The first of these products multiplies two transpositions and is even, so that slot cannot finish as an odd four-cycle. Thus at least four products are required.

In fact the product count is odd. Reduce the ordered exponent matrix modulo two. Its starting value has rows $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$, and the target is the identity. The starting matrix and each elementary row addition transpose two of the three nonzero vectors of $\mathbb F_2^2$. Inversion and conjugation leave the matrix unchanged. Taking permutation signs gives an odd number of row additions, hence at least five products. At least one inversion is needed because both initial $y$-exponents are negative and right additions keep them negative. Each inversion changes the sign of the determinant of the exponent matrix. Both endpoint determinants are $+1$, so there must be at least two inversions. With the two necessary conjugations, every path has at least nine moves. Our path attains all three lower bounds.

**A uniform obstruction.** Fix $n\ge1$ and let $m=n(n+1)+2\ge4$. Use the following determinant-one matrices modulo $m$, identifying each matrix with its negative.

$$X=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Y=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

They satisfy $XYX=YXY$. The matrix $M=X^nY^{-(n+1)}$ has rows $(1+n(n+1),n)$ and $(n+1,1)$. Its determinant is one and its trace vanishes modulo $m$. The two-by-two characteristic identity gives $M^2=-I$ over this ring, including composite $m$. Thus the initial relators have images $(s,1)$ with $s^2=1$.

If there is at most one conjugation, every target relator lies in a subgroup generated by $s$ and a conjugate $t$ of $s$ (take $t=s$ for zero conjugations). All squares in this subgroup commute. Indeed, putting $r=st$, every element is $r^k$ or $r^ks$, since $srs=r^{-1}$. Squaring gives $r^{2k}$ or $1$. If the path ended at the standard pair, this would force $X^2$ and $(XY)^2$ to commute. But the lower-right entries of $X^2(XY)^2$ and $(XY)^2X^2$ are respectively $0$ and $-2$. These differ even up to sign modulo $m$. The Lean proof detects this on the class of the vector $(0,1)$ modulo simultaneous negation.

**Adding the length bound.** Given a path with $c$ conjugations and total length $L$, erase the conjugations and replay every inversion and multiplication on the same slots. Induction on the move list shows that both paths have identical exponent-sum matrices after corresponding prefixes. If the original endpoint is $(x,y)$, the shortened path therefore reaches a pair with identity exponent matrix in $L-c$ moves. ACC-P000032 gives at least $n+2$ products and at least two non-product moves on the shortened path. Those latter moves are all inversions. Hence $L-c\ge n+4$. Since $c\ge2$, we obtain $L\ge n+6$. [Hideto Iitsuka, ACC-P000032](https://competition.sair.foundation/contributor-network/acc/ACC-P000032)

**Context and verification.** Akbulut and Kirby introduced the family. Borovik, Khukhro and Myasnikov proposed using finite Andrews–Curtis graphs, and Borovik, Lubotzky and Myasnikov determined their connected components. The finite images here impose a lower bound on paths with a restricted count of one operation. Oancea's thesis, §7.2, gives quantitative results for the Akbulut–Kirby family using M-transformations. Havas and Ramsay studied the case $n=2$ with single-letter conjugators and also considered changes to the initial and terminal representatives.
