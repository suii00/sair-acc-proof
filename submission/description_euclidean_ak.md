**Theorem.** For every integer $n\geq 0$, the minimum number of directed ordinary Andrews–Curtis moves taking the ordered Akbulut–Kirby pair

$$R_n=(x^n y^{-(n+1)},\;xyx(yxy)^{-1})$$

to a pair with identity exponent-sum matrix is exactly $n+4$. The minimum number of right multiplications is $n+2$, even when conjugations and inversions are free for that count. A single path attains both minima. In particular, every ordinary path from $R_n$ to the ordered standard pair $(x,y)$ has at least $n+4$ moves. Whether such a trivialization exists in general remains open.

**Move convention.** Work in the rank-two free group. One move in the official `AC.Step` inverts one relator, conjugates one relator by an arbitrary word, or right-multiplies one relator by the other. Consequently, exponent rows may be negated or added, and conjugations fix the matrix. The order of the rows, their signs, the direction of the path, and the cost of a positive right multiplication are all part of the statement. Stabilizations are outside its scope. [Official definitions at the pinned revision](https://github.com/SAIRcompetition/Andrews-Curtis/blob/a0fd6e6f52d82c93ccc06ab91d81e8fa3678256e/competition/tools/lean/AC.lean)

**A lower bound from a character.** For $a,b\geq0$ let $D(a,b)$ be the number of times one must subtract the smaller from the larger until one coordinate is zero. Set $D=0$ on the axes. Equal positive coordinates need one last subtraction. The sum decreases at each subtraction, $D$ is symmetric, and $D(a+b,b)=1+D(a,b)$ for $b>0$. In particular $D(a,1)=a$.

For any homomorphism $\chi:F(x,y)\to\mathbb Z$, apply $D$ to the absolute values of the two relator images. Inversion and conjugation preserve the result. Adding a signed coordinate to the other changes it by at most one. To see this, let $a,b$ be the magnitudes of the changed and fixed coordinates, respectively. If $b=0$ nothing changes. For equal signs use the addition identity. For opposite signs with $a\geq b>0$, use it in reverse. If $0<a<b$, the old pair $(a,b)$ and new pair $(b-a,b)$ each reduce in one step to the same unordered pair. When $a=0<b$ the change is from the axis to $(b,b)$. Summing over a path bounds the difference in endpoint potentials by the number of right multiplications.

**Why the matrix needs one more addition.** With rows indexed by the two relators and columns by $x,y$, the initial matrix is

$$A_n=\begin{pmatrix} n&-(n+1)\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its $y$-column has depth $n+1$, while the identity's $y$-column has depth zero. Suppose exactly $n+1$ additions suffice. Every addition must lower that depth by one. Before the first addition *into the second row*, its two entries still have absolute value one. Write $a$ for the magnitude of the first row's $y$-entry at that time. If $a\geq2$, the new second magnitude is $a-1$ or $a+1$. The corresponding depths are $D(a,a-1)=a$ and $D(a,a+1)=a+1$, both at least the old depth $D(a,1)=a$. If $a=0$, the depth is already zero. If $a=1$, a decrease must make the second $y$-entry zero, leaving depth zero. No more additions are possible under the assumed count, and inversion or conjugation cannot take that entry to the required $1$. Finally, if no addition ever targets the second row, its $x$-entry still has magnitude one, whereas the target requires zero. These cases give at least $n+2$ additions.

The initial and final determinants are both $+1$. Each inversion flips their sign along the path, so the number of inversions is even. The initial $y$-column is nonpositive and adding rows preserves nonpositivity. Reaching the positive entry of the target therefore takes at least two inversions. Together these bounds give $n+4$ moves.

**Attainment.** Invert the second row to obtain $(-1,1)$. Add it to the first row $n+1$ times to obtain $(-1,0)$. Invert this first row, then add it once to the second row. The result is the ordered identity. These are actual relator operations starting from $R_n$, so they reach a free-word pair with those exponent sums. The first theorem below certifies both minima for this target set. The second gives the lower bound for the particular target $(x,y)$. For example, $R_3$ takes exactly seven moves to the target set, and any ordinary trivialization of $R_3$ requires at least seven.

**Relation to earlier work.** Akbulut and Kirby introduced the family. Riley's Proposition 3.2 proves that a signed integer pair $(1,k)$ requires $k$ additions or subtractions to reach an axis. It accounts for the initial one-column estimate. Mulay's SAIR contribution uses exponent matrices and the Farey tree for a lower bound on a different Miller–Schupp construction. Thakur also uses exponent-matrix distances computationally. Here the equality case for the $y$-column, the ordered second row, and determinant parity yield an exact formula for this directed Akbulut–Kirby matrix problem.
